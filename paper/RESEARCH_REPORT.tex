\begin{abstract}
This document collects every empirical finding from this paper in one place:
what was measured, what the numbers are, and what they mean. It is written
for readers who want the substance without the methodological scaffolding.
\end{abstract}

%% ============================================================
\section{The Central Question}
%% ============================================================

Agent leaderboards report task success and dollar cost. Practitioners pick a
``cost-efficient'' system from one benchmark and assume it stays efficient
elsewhere. We test that assumption.

Three things get conflated in every unconditioned cost number: the model's
price, the benchmark's difficulty, and the scaffold's execution behavior.
Disentangling them is the paper's entire project.

%% ============================================================
\section{What We Studied}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Primary data}

242 agent evaluations from the Holistic Agent Leaderboard (HAL): 9 benchmarks,
11 scaffolds, 29 model labels. Dollar cost and accuracy are available; tokens,
retries, cache behavior, and per-task trajectories are not.

The only scaffold repeated broadly enough is the HAL Generalist Agent. Six
benchmarks have enough shared labels for analysis: CORE-Bench Hard, GAIA,
SciCode, ScienceAgentBench, SWE-bench Verified Mini, and TAU-bench Airline.
This gives 86 rows and 25 distinct model labels.

\subsection{Matched trace data}

977 exact task-model pairs from Open-SWE-Traces (NVIDIA): MiniMax-M2.5 under
two scaffolds (OpenHands and SWE-agent) on identical tasks. Resolved status,
trajectory turns, tool calls, and message characters are available. Tokens and
dollars are not.

\subsection{Telemetry data}

Two public coding-agent telemetry corpora: TraceLab (8{,}058 real sessions,
665{,}453 LLM rounds, 743{,}819 tool calls) and MIMO Claude Code (1{,}017
sessions, 4{,}690 rounds, one model). Neither has task outcomes or billing.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 1: Raw Cost Ranks Transfer}
%% ============================================================

Across the six high-overlap benchmark pairs, raw dollar-cost Spearman
correlations range from 0.74 to 0.93, with a mean of 0.84. All survive
permutation testing and Holm correction.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Benchmark pair & Shared labels & Spearman $\rho$ \\
\midrule
GAIA -- SWE-mini & 17 & 0.89 \\
CORE -- GAIA & 14 & 0.93 \\
CORE -- TAU & 14 & 0.88 \\
GAIA -- TAU & 13 & 0.93 \\
CORE -- SWE-mini & 12 & 0.74 \\
SWE-mini -- TAU & 12 & 0.86 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A practitioner using a GAIA-like leaderboard to rank raw spend for a SWE-like
workload would get a useful prior.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 2: But the Signal Is Mostly Label Propensity}
%% ============================================================

A pooled model---$\log C_{i,b} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_b + \epsilon_{i,b}$,
where $\alpha_i$ is a displayed-label fixed effect and $\beta_b$ is a
benchmark effect---explains 82.0\% of observed cost variation (adjusted
$R^2 = 72.7\%$) on 86 rows with 30 parameters.

After removing label and benchmark cost propensity, \textbf{no residual-cost
rank association is significant} under a 20{,}000-draw permutation test.

The portable raw-cost signal is largely a stable observed label-level
component. The public data cannot identify whether that component comes from
provider pricing, reasoning settings, routing, token behavior, or cache
treatment.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 3: Success Adjustment Changes the Ranking}
%% ============================================================

Aggregate dollars per expected success (CPS) reorders labels: mean CPS
correlation is 0.59, compared with 0.84 for raw cost. Only three CPS
associations survive permutation testing and Holm correction:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item CORE--GAIA: $\rho = 0.87$, 95\% interval [0.63, 0.96]
\item CORE--TAU: 0.83, [0.31, 1.00]
\item GAIA--TAU: 0.78, [0.42, 0.94]
\end{itemize}

The clearest gap is GAIA--SWE-mini: raw cost exceeds CPS correlation by 0.60,
with a 95\% interval [0.14, 1.13]. A label that is predictably cheap is not
necessarily predictably cheap per expected success on a different workload.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 4: Held-Out Prediction Works for Four Workloads}
%% ============================================================

Leave-one-benchmark-out (LOBO) prediction: learn each label's cost propensity
from the other workloads, predict the held-out cost rank.

\textbf{Strong prediction:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item CORE: $\rho = 0.78$, $N = 14$, $p = 0.0016$
\item GAIA: 0.87, $N = 16$, $p < 0.0001$
\item SWE-mini: 0.90, $N = 16$, $p < 0.0001$
\item TAU: 0.83, $N = 14$, $p = 0.0004$
\end{itemize}

Leave-one-label-out ranges remain positive for every omission.

\textbf{Failed prediction:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item SciCode: unstable, $[-0.50, 0.14]$
\item ScienceAgentBench: directionally negative, $[-0.77, -0.43]$
\end{itemize}

SciCode has very low mean accuracy (2.1\%) and one-third zero-accuracy labels.
ScienceAgentBench has the narrowest log-cost dispersion.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 5: No Universal Cost-Accuracy Winner}
%% ============================================================

No label tested on at least five benchmarks appears on every accuracy-cost
frontier under any of three definitions: discrete nondominance, the HAL-inspired
hull, or a 5\% tolerance. The stronger claim that no label appears on more than
4/6 frontiers fails under tolerance: o4-mini Low reaches 5/6. We retain only
the non-universal-winner conclusion.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 6: Scaffolds Change Execution Burden}
%% ============================================================

Across 977 matched task-model pairs (MiniMax-M2.5), OpenHands uses
substantially fewer resources than SWE-agent at the same task:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Metric & SWE-agent median & OpenHands median & Ratio (OH/SA) \\
\midrule
Trajectory turns & 149 & 123 & 0.85 [0.82, 0.87] \\
Assistant turns / tool calls & 74 & 61 & 0.85 [0.82, 0.86] \\
Total characters & 194{,}263 & 176{,}708 & 0.95 [0.92, 0.98] \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

OpenHands is lower on 69.7\% of pairs for turns and tool calls. Resolved
status has no detected difference ($p = 0.197$).

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 7: The Failure-Cost Premium Is a Composition Artifact}
%% ============================================================

This is the paper's most counterintuitive finding.

\textbf{The naive view (what prior work reports):} Comparing each scaffold's
failed versus resolved trajectories across tasks, failures look 19--36\%
heavier. OpenHands turns ratio: 1.21 [1.07, 1.36]. SWE-agent trajectory
characters: 1.33 [1.11, 1.54]. This matches the ``expensive failure'' claim
in prior resource-effectiveness work.

\textbf{The task-matched view (what happens when you hold the task fixed):}
Among 107 pairs where one scaffold resolved and the other failed on the
\emph{identical} task:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item The failed side is heavier only 47--52 times out of 107 on every metric
  ($p \geq 0.25$, two-sided binomial)
\item After normalizing each trajectory by its own scaffold's median burden,
  the failed/resolved ratio is 0.98--1.06 with intervals spanning 1
\item 95\% confidence intervals: [0.37, 0.57] raw, [0.43, 0.63] normalized
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Power analysis:} With 107 pairs, a two-sided binomial test at
$\alpha = 0.05$ has 80\% power to detect a premium fraction of 0.65 or larger
(failed heavier on 70+ pairs). The observed 0.47--0.53 falls well below this
threshold, ruling out strong premiums ($>$15 percentage points) but not modest
ones.

\textbf{Interpretation:} Hard tasks make agents both burn more and fail. The
observed cross-task ``failure premium'' is explained by which tasks failed, not
by failure adding burden. Unconditioned failure-cost ratios measure workload
composition, not a property of failing runs.

\textbf{Scope:} One model (MiniMax-M2.5), four-shard nonrandom sample,
character/turn proxies not tokens or dollars.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 8: Agent Inference Is Context-Dominated}
%% ============================================================

Across two independent telemetry sources, observable inference volume is
dominated by repeated carried context, not fresh generation.

\textbf{TraceLab (real sessions):}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Median round: 132{,}092 input tokens, 249 output tokens
\item Mean cache-read share: 93.7\%
\item Median session context growth: 47{,}267 tokens; P90 205{,}248
\item Median final-round context: $3.6\times$ initial (P90 $9.9\times$)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{MIMO Claude Code (one model):}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Corpus totals: 46.3M uncached input, 753.0M cache-read, 12.0M output
  tokens
\item Cache-read volume is $16.3\times$ uncached input
\end{itemize}

A benchmark's single dollar total hides whether spending comes from model
price, cache policy, context growth, or fresh generation.

%% ============================================================

In TraceLab:

\midrule

The top 1\% of sessions hold 48.3\% of summed input tokens. The top 10\% hold

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 10: Error Spirals and Retry Behavior}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Cascade:} After a successful tool call, the next call errs 4.9\% of
the time. After an erroneous call, 21.0\% ($4.29\times$ conditional increase).

\textbf{Retry:} 88.3\% of errored tool calls are followed by another call to
the same tool in the same session. 77.9\% of those immediate retries succeed.
Per-call recovery is common.

\textbf{Spiral tails:} Consecutive-error runs have median length 1 but P90
length 3 and a maximum of 368. Median successful run length is 9.

\textbf{Cross-source replication:} The MIMO corpus replicates the cascade
ratio almost exactly: 4.3\% after an error versus 1.0\% after a success
($4.33\times$), despite a different model and source.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 11: Tool Failures Are Slower and Less Informative}
%% ============================================================

Across 743{,}819 TraceLab tool calls:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Successful calls: median latency 140 ms, median result 602 characters
\item Failed calls: median latency 313 ms, median result 208 characters
\item P95 latency: 85.6s (failed) vs 15.8s (success)
\end{itemize}

Failures consume more wall time while yielding less information.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 12: Large Tool Outputs Grow Context}
%% ============================================================

When the current round contains under 1k tool-result characters, median
next-input growth is 443 tokens. For 1k--10k characters it is 1{,}412 tokens;
for over 10k it is 5{,}420 tokens. Tool verbosity is a concrete mechanism
linking tool use to subsequent context burden.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 13: MIMO Has a Data-Quality Issue}
%% ============================================================

In every one of the 1{,}017 released MIMO sessions, the per-round usage block
repeats one constant tuple: identical uncached input, cache creation, cache
read, and output across all rounds of a session. Within-session token dynamics
cannot be validated in this source.

The source is still useful for session counts, corpus totals, cross-session
concentration (top 10\% hold 37.9\% of summed input), and content-derived
error sequences. It is not usable for round-level growth analysis.

%% ============================================================
\section{Finding 14: Public Telemetry Is Fragmented}
%% ============================================================

No public source jointly exposes task, model, scaffold, outcome, tokens, cache
use, retries, latency, and billing.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{2cm}p{5cm}p{5cm}}
\toprule
Source & Has & Lacks \\
\midrule
HAL & Accuracy, dollar cost, task, model, scaffold & Tokens, traces, retries \\
TraceLab & Tokens, cache, tools, sessions & Task outcomes, dollars \\
MIMO & Tokens, cache, tools, sessions & Task outcomes, dollars, round fidelity \\
Open-SWE & Task, model, scaffold, resolved status & Tokens, dollars \\
Exgentic & Multi-benchmark usage, OTel spans & Rewards, task matching \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

This fragmentation motivates a minimum public telemetry standard for agent
leaderboards.

%% ============================================================
\section{Summary}
%% ============================================================

The paper's thesis is now supported by three independent lines of evidence:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Cost ranks transfer, but it is mostly label propensity.} The
  portable signal is real ($\rho = 0.74$--$0.93$) but largely absorbed by a
  fixed-effects model; residual ranks are insignificant.

\item \textbf{Success adjustment changes the ranking.} CPS is less portable
  (mean $\rho = 0.59$), and only three pairs survive correction.

\item \textbf{The failure-cost premium is a composition artifact.} At fixed
  task and model, failed trajectories carry the same burden as successful ones.
  Hard tasks make agents burn more and fail; unconditioned failure-cost ratios
  measure workload composition.
\end{enumerate}

Every unconditioned aggregate---raw cost, success-adjusted efficiency, failure
premiums---conflates which workloads were attempted with how agents behaved on
them.
